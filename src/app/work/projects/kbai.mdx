## Project Overview

The Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus (ARC) benchmark was proposed by François Chollet (Google Deepmind and creator of Keras) in 2019. 
The benchmark is built not as a concrete but more a suggestion of the concept underlying that unless AI (or whatever model we reference
to as AI) can solve novel unseen and untrained upon problems with minimal to no examples, we cannot say that Artificial General Intelligence
(AGI) has been achieved. 

The benchmark is built to test reasoning in its most distilled form. Each task presents a small set of colored grids.
Some are paired as examples of an input transformed into an output, others are left for the solver to complete
without guidance. Despite the minimal format, the challenges require pattern recognition, spatial reasoning, and abstraction all
at once. It’s the kind of puzzle that 5-year-old humans can solve visually and intuitively through pattern matching,
but for a machine it demands careful engineering to make it approachable .

For my Knowledge-Based AI course at Georgia Tech, I designed a rudimentary agent with basic heuristics that could interpret these problems
through a modular reasoning pipeline. It processes each task by breaking down the grid into identifiable structures, 
testing a library of transformations, and using its accumulated successes during the "training" examples to guide its choices on unseen case(s).
The goal was to make a system that could adapt within the constraints of rule-based logic, operating on principles that could be traced and understood.

## Designing the Agent

Each problem begins with an analysis of the training examples. The agent parses the grids to identify shapes, detect boundaries, 
assess color distributions, and note the spatial arrangement of objects. This creates a working profile of the task that guides 
its approach. Transformations in its library include geometric changes like rotation and reflection, logical operations such as 
XOR or masking, and operations on individual objects like duplication or recoloring.

These transformations are applied in two stages. First, the agent tests them on the entire grid, looking for easy and lazy matches to the 
training outputs. Then, it uses object detection libraries to segment the grid into connected components and re-run the same transformations on each piece 
independently. This dual-step search, whole-grid and object-focused, covers both global transformations and cases where separate parts 
of the grid follow different rules and results in solving 61/96 problems.

Each transformation that produces a correct match is recorded. Over the course of the problem, the agent builds a ranked list 
of strategies weighted by their frequency of success. This ranking is then used during inference to select the transformations 
most likely to solve the test case. Multiple outputs can be generated in this stage to account for uncertainty. We then select the
prediction with the highest probability of success of matching with the training outputs.

## Results and Observations

The agent was tested on 96 ARC problems divided across three stages. In Milestone B, where training and test cases 
share strong similarities, it solved 15/16 tasks. Milestone C introduced more abstract variations, with 12/16
 solved. Milestone D contained the most complex tasks—often requiring multi-step reasoning or manipulation of nested 
structures—and the agent succeeded in 6/16.

Several solutions illustrate the agent’s strengths. In one problem, it segmented the grid, applied a diagonal extension 
heuristic, and mapped colors based on patterns learned from the examples.

## Challenges

Ambiguous color mappings were one of the most consistent sources of error. When the relationship between input and output colors 
shifted based on context, the current mapping logic often misinterpreted the transformation. Nested hollow shapes also posed 
difficulties for enclosure detection, sometimes resulting in incorrect fills. Another limitation was the agent’s commitment to 
its ranked list of transformations once it moved into inference. If an approach was unlikely to work, the system had no mechanism 
to re-evaluate and adjust mid-task.

These challenges were most pronounced in the Milestone D set, where the problems often required reasoning steps that fell outside 
the transformations the agent had learned to prioritize. In this step, one thing I realized was that the order of transformations
matters just as much if not the most to the chaining of transformations. This ended up being a heuristic I was unable to solve and
the sole location where I relied on a somewhat semi-brute force approach.

## Future Work

Further development would focus on refining the color inference to handle ambiguity instead of frequency, improving enclosure detection for more complex 
spatial structures, and adding a meta-reasoning process/heuristic that allows the agent to shift strategies when initial attempts fail in a smarter search for solutions. 
These additions would give it more flexibility in handling the kinds of abstract, multi-layered tasks that are still out of reach.

I would also want to focus on creating an appropriately complex transformation chaining heuristic that utilizes some notion of a distance
function to make the agent more flexible and smarter in terms of not wasting time on transformation chains that will yield no proper result.
One such idea I did not have the time to implement was a markov chain and/or a bidirectional graph based approach to keep track of transformations
and reduce the search space as much as possible by working backwards from the output to the input. I hypothesize that this approach will take
fewer and smarter steps towards the solution.

While this implementation stops short of human-level adaptability, it demonstrates that a rudimentary, modular, and interpretable reasoning system 
can achieve meaningful progress on a benchmark as challenging as ARC. The process of building it also reinforced how much of 
problem solving comes from balancing consistent structure with the ability to adjust when the structure no longer applies.
