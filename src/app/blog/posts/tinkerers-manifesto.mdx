Most people treat learning like a finish line. You grind through school, land the job, pick up the tools the role requires, and somewhere along the way the learning quietly tapers off. Not because you stopped caring (at least not at first) but because the pressure to produce starts crowding out the space to explore. That is the trap. And the engineers who fall into it fastest are often the most focused on getting somewhere.

I want to push back on that.

## What Tinkering Actually Means

Tinkering gets a bad reputation. People hear the word and picture a bedroom floor covered in disassembled electronics or a GitHub graveyard of half finished repos that never shipped. And sure, I have one of those (the graveyard, not the bedroom floor... well, both). But that framing misses what tinkering actually is when done with intention.

Real tinkering is deliberate curiosity without a predetermined outcome. The goal is not to build something polished. The goal is to follow a question somewhere interesting.

When I was working through a graduate level computer science course at Georgia Tech, I ran into a genuinely frustrating problem. I needed a study tool that could answer questions about my course materials, but the environment I was working in had no internet access. No GPT, no search, nothing. So I got to tinkering. I built a local RAG (retrieval augmented generation) system from scratch, running entirely on my machine, that could ingest my notes and answer questions about them. No rubric. No assignment. Just a problem, a pile of documentation, and a weekend of figuring things out.

That project taught me more about vector embeddings, chunking strategies, and the mechanics of language models than any course module ever could have. More importantly, it changed how I think. When I hit a wall now, my first instinct is to build my way through it.

(The RAG system still lives on my machine, by the way. I still use it.)

## Why GenAI Changes the Stakes

The landscape in AI engineering does not sit still. Every few months there is a new model, a new framework, a new paradigm the whole field shifts toward. If you are waiting for a course to catch up to the frontier, you are already behind.

The engineers who are genuinely dangerous in this environment are the ones who built something with a new tool the week it dropped. They read the release notes, spun up a proof of concept, hit the weird edge cases, and figured out what the thing is actually good at before anyone else formed an opinion. Passive consumption (watching the explainers, skimming the posts) keeps you informed in a shallow way. Building with something makes you fluent.

I built an AI engineering team simulation not because anyone asked me to, but because the question would not leave me alone. What if you could spin up an entire software engineering organization with agents? What would the project manager agent need to know? How would you handle disagreement between a frontend agent and a backend agent? That question pulled me deep into multi agent orchestration, context window management, and system design problems I would never have encountered just reading about them.

(Curiosity is a better teacher than curriculum. That is not a metaphor.)

## The Creativity Connection

Here is the thing about tinkering that people underestimate. You can tinker mindlessly, clicking around tools without really engaging, and you will get almost nothing out of it. The ingredient that transforms tinkering into real learning is creativity.

Creativity in engineering is not about making things pretty. It is about asking the weird question at the right moment. It is the leap you take when you look at two unrelated things and think, what if I connected these? What if I used this tool in a way the documentation never suggested? What if the constraint is the most interesting part of the problem?

That creative leap is what separates builders from users. Users learn what the tools can do. Builders figure out what the tools should do next. In a world where the tools change every quarter, being a builder is not optional anymore. It is the whole job. Engineering has always required creativity, but GenAI raised the floor on what that creativity needs to look like. Knowing the API is table stakes. Knowing what to build with it is the actual skill.

## What Happens When You Stop

I want to be honest without being dramatic about it. The gap between someone who tinkers regularly and someone who coasts does not stay the same size. It grows. And it grows faster now than it did five years ago.

Coasting used to mean you were a little behind on syntax updates or missed a framework release. Now it can mean you missed an entire architectural shift in how production AI systems are built. (That is not a scare tactic. That is just what the last two years looked like.)

The gap is not always visible until it is. And by then, closing it takes real effort.

## The Manifesto

So here is what I try to hold onto. Build things you have no business building yet. Ask questions that do not have Stack Overflow answers. Pick up a tool that just dropped and break it before anyone has written a tutorial about it. Let your side projects be weird and unfinished and genuinely exploratory rather than safe and designed to impress.

The engineers who stayed curious when it would have been easier to stop are the ones who matter five years from now. School gives you a foundation. Curiosity builds everything on top of it.

If I could tell myself something at 21, it would be this: you will never arrive at a point where you have learned enough. The learning does not end at graduation. It barely starts there.

Stay in the graveyard. Keep building. The mess is the point.
