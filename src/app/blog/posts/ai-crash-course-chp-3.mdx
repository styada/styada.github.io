Every technology has a mythology. For AI, the mythology started in the 1950s: men in suits imagined machines that could think like humans, and they believed it was just around the corner. It wasn't. What followed was a fascinating, humbling 70-year journey of brilliant ideas, broken promises, winter after winter, and finally — maybe — a spring.

## The Dream

Alan Turing asked the right question back in 1950: "Can machines think?" He didn't claim to answer it, but he proposed a test — the famous Turing Test. If a machine could fool you into thinking you were talking to a human, well, that's pretty close to thinking, isn't it?

That question lit a fire. In the summer of 1956, a small group of researchers gathered at Dartmouth College with an audacious goal: create artificial intelligence within the summer. They genuinely believed they could solve it. John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, and others were convinced that intelligence was something you could program into a machine, just like you'd write any other code.

The early AI systems were impressive for the time. They could play checkers. They could solve algebra problems. Researchers published papers with titles like "Machines that Think." The potential felt limitless. Funding poured in. Everyone wanted a piece of AI.

Then reality got in the way.

## The First Winter

It turned out that "intelligence" wasn't as simple as they'd thought. The problems were harder, the solutions required more computing power, and the systems that worked in labs didn't generalize well to the real world. By the late 1970s, the hype had deflated. Funding dried up. The term "AI" became something of a curse — if you wanted money for research, you stopped calling it AI and started calling it "information systems" or something else.

This period, from the early 1970s to the early 1980s, is called the First AI Winter. It lasted years. Researchers who'd promised machines that could think were now struggling to fund their labs.

## The Boom-and-Bust Cycle

But AI didn't die. It evolved. In the 1980s, expert systems emerged — AI trained to mimic the decision-making of human experts in narrow domains like medicine or geology. They worked well enough that companies invested heavily. The funding came back. Enthusiasm returned.

Then, predictably, the limitations became clear. Expert systems were brittle. They couldn't handle situations their creators didn't anticipate. Another winter came, this time in the late 1980s and early 1990s.

This pattern — hype, promise, disappointment, winter — repeated for decades. Each time, researchers learned something, built better techniques, but the gap between what they could do and what people expected remained.

## The Seeds of the Current Era

Two things changed the equation.

First, data became abundant. The internet meant billions of examples of text, images, and interaction logs. Where researchers once had thousands of examples, they now had millions or billions. AI systems trained on that much data could learn richer, more nuanced patterns.

Second, computing power exploded. GPUs, which were designed for video games, turned out to be perfect for training certain kinds of AI models. The cost and time to train big models started dropping. What took weeks on expensive hardware could now happen faster, cheaper.

Third — well, that's more than two, but bear with me — the algorithms got better. In 2017, a breakthrough called the Transformer architecture emerged. It turned out to be exceptionally good at learning from sequences of data. Text is a sequence. Images can be thought of as sequences. Time series data is a sequence. The Transformer could handle all of it.

Combine those three things and suddenly the AI that researchers had been pursuing for 70 years started actually working.

## Why Now Feels Different

The models of the last few years — transformers trained on massive datasets running on modern hardware — produce outputs that surprised even their creators. They write coherently. They reason about novel problems. They can learn from a handful of examples. They fail in weird and specific ways, but when they succeed, it's genuinely impressive.

This isn't different because we've finally achieved consciousness or general AI. We haven't. It's different because the gap between what the systems can do and what people experience has gotten small enough that it actually matters in daily life.

Looking back at the history, you can see why previous cycles of hype ended in disappointment: the gap was too wide. People expected too much. The systems delivered too little. But now, even with honest limitations, the systems deliver enough that they're genuinely useful.

That doesn't mean we're done with hype or disappointment. New winters might still come. But they'll probably come from us asking more rather than from the technology failing to deliver what it promised.
